# THE GHOST SHIP OF THE STRAITS
## The True Mystery of the SS *Ourang Medan*

**Format:** Deep-dive investigative documentary / video essay
**Target runtime:** ~60:00
**Narration:** ~6,500 words at ~135 wpm, plus scored visual-only beats and ~14 s of visual-only transition per scene (computed runtime ≈ 59:20)
**Tone:** A cinematic thriller with an investigator's discipline. It tells the legend at full intensity, then puts it on trial.

---

## STYLE BIBLE (applies to every scene)

**Two visual tracks.** This is the spine of the film.
| Track | Used for | Palette | Texture |
|---|---|---|---|
| **THE LEGEND** | Events as the story tells them | Ink-black, bone-white, a single **blood-red** accent | A graphic-novel look: heavy shadows, hard rim light, film grain |
| **THE RECORD** | What documents actually show | Charcoal, paper-cream, **archival amber** | Scanned paper, halftone dots, typewriter type, desk-lamp glow |

The audience must always know which track they're on. Switching tracks is a storytelling event. Mark it with a **"tape-stop" transition**: the frame desaturates, the audio pitch drops, and a hard cut follows.

**Vector art.** Flat 2.5D vector shapes separated into parallax layers (foreground, midground, background) for depth. Characters are silhouettes with minimal facial detail. Horror is implied through posture and light, never gore.

**Motion grammar.**
- *Push-ins* for dread. *Snap-zooms* for revelations. *Whip-pans* to change location.
- Kinetic typography: key words slam into frame on percussion hits, then hold for 12 frames.
- Maps are a recurring "home base". A dark 3D-tilted map of the strait returns in every chapter, with new layers added each time.
- The **Morse motif**: dots and dashes visualized as pulses of light. They appear as a recurring transition element.

**Sound.** Low drones, metallic hull groans, and real CW (Morse) tones near 500 kHz-era radio texture. Silence is used as a weapon.

**Integrity rules.**
- Any "CLASSIFIED / TOP SECRET" stamp is stylized and **never** shown on a realistic-looking document attributed to a real agency.
- A one-time disclaimer appears in Chapter 6.
- Lines tagged **[VERIFY]** must be confirmed against primary sources before picture lock (see Appendix A).

## CHAPTER 1: THE FUNNEL [02:38 – 08:07]

### Scene 1.1: The Most Important Water on Earth (02:38 – 03:49)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- The **home-base map** is introduced: a dark, 3D-tilted vector map of Southeast Asia. The camera begins at orbital height and drops in a fast, smooth arc toward the strait.
- Sumatra (left) and the Malay Peninsula (right) rise as extruded landmasses. Singapore is a glowing node at the southern tip.
- Shipping lanes appear as luminous threads converging into the strait, like sand flowing into an hourglass neck. Animated "particle ships" stream through it.
- Kinetic text: **THE STRAIT OF MALACCA** → **~900 KM** → **INDIAN OCEAN ⇄ SOUTH CHINA SEA**.
- **Audio:** a rising orchestral pulse, overlaid with distant ships' horns.

**Narration:**
> To understand this mystery, you have to understand the stage it played out on.
>
> The Strait of Malacca is a long, narrow corridor of water, roughly nine hundred kilometres long, squeezed between the island of Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula. On a map it looks like a crack between two landmasses. In reality it's one of the most important pieces of water on the planet.
>
> Almost everything moving between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific has to pass through this funnel. For more than a thousand years it has carried spices, silk, tin, rubber and, later, oil. Empires have risen and fallen fighting over who controls it.
>
> And in the years right after the Second World War, it was one of the most chaotic places on Earth.

>
> The Netherlands refused to accept it. What followed was a brutal four-year war. In July 1947, Dutch forces launched a major military offensive, and the Dutch navy tried to choke the young Republic with a naval blockade.
>
> Across the strait, British Malaya was heading into its own crisis. By June 1948, the colonial government had declared a state of emergency against a communist insurgency.
>
> So picture these waters in 1947 and 1948. Warships patrolling. Ports changing hands. Paperwork, registries and colonial authority all in pieces.

### Scene 1.3: The Shadow Fleet (04:58 – 06:58)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- The map darkens. Small vector boats with no lights, rendered as hollow outlines, slip between the patrol lines. Each carries an icon: a crate of rifles, rubber bales, an opium poppy, and a barrel marked with a stylized hazard symbol.
- The camera follows one dark boat in a tracking shot as it threads past a patrol searchlight sweep.
- The final frame shows the hazard barrel in close-up. A slow push-in, and the symbol glows red.
- **Audio:** a tense, low string ostinato, plus a searchlight "hum".

**Narration:**
> And wherever there's a blockade, there are smugglers.
>
> The strait filled with blockade runners: small, fast boats and rusting freighters carrying rubber, weapons, fuel and medicine. Some flew false flags. Some carried false names. Some didn't appear in any registry at all, and that was the point.
>
> Meanwhile, the war had left something else behind. Military stockpiles were scattered across the region: munitions, fuel and industrial chemicals that nobody had fully catalogued, and that were worth a fortune to the right buyer.
>
> This is a world where a ship could sail without a name. Where a cargo could be lied about. And where, if something went wrong out at sea, nobody official might ever hear about it.
>
> And this wasn't small-time crime. For the young Indonesian Republic, cut off by the Dutch blockade, smuggling was a lifeline. Produce from Sumatra (rubber, and by many accounts even opium) was run across the strait to Singapore and sold to buy weapons and supplies. **[VERIFY]** Ships slipped out of small Sumatran harbours at night, ran the patrols, and came back loaded with whatever the war needed.
>
> On the other side, Dutch warships stopped and searched what they could catch. Crews were arrested. Cargoes were seized. Some boats simply never came home, and nobody filed a report, because there was no one to file it with.
>
> Keep that in mind. Because one night, according to the story, something went very wrong.

### Scene 1.4: The Men at the Key (06:58 – 08:07)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- A vector "character study" of a 1940s ship's radio officer: a silhouette at a desk, a cigarette ember, a logbook, a clock showing the hour with two red wedges marked at :15–:18 and :45–:48.
- A close-up of a hand on the key. Its rhythm visualized as a unique "fingerprint" waveform that animates beside it.
- **Audio:** a clock tick that syncs with the Morse rhythm.

**Narration:**
> One last thing before the signal comes, because you need to understand the man who sent it.
>
> Merchant ships of this era carried a radio officer, often nicknamed "Sparks". Day and night, the most important frequency on his set was the international distress frequency for Morse traffic, 500 kilohertz. Twice an hour, every station on Earth was supposed to go quiet for three minutes, at quarter past and quarter to the hour, so that weak distress signals could be heard.
>
> And here's a detail that matters later. Experienced operators could recognise each other by their *fist*: the personal rhythm of how they tapped the key. A steady fist meant a steady hand. When a fist started to fall apart, the men listening could hear it.

## CHAPTER 2: THE SIGNAL [08:07 – 14:11]

### Scene 2.1: The Listening Posts (08:07 – 09:31)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- Back to the **LEGEND track**. A radio room interior, built as a retro-futuristic composite: 1940s hardware (Bakelite dials, brass key, valve receiver glowing orange) with modern holographic data overlays floating over it.
- Lighting: green CRT and valve glow from below, with deep shadows. A single desk lamp.
- A silhouetted operator in headphones leans in. The camera does a slow 180° orbit around him.
- A frequency dial animates as a glowing ring sweeping across its band.
- Kinetic text in the corner: **DATE: DISPUTED** (it flickers between *JUNE 1947*, *LATE 1947* and *FEB 1948*).
- **Audio:** the room tone of a valve receiver, soft static, a ticking clock.

**Narration:**
> The exact date depends on which version you read. Some say June 1947. Others say February 1948. That alone should make us cautious. But let's tell the story the way it has always been told.
>
> Along the coasts of the strait, radio operators at Dutch and British stations are listening to the maritime bands. It's tedious work: hours of weather reports, position signals and routine traffic, all coming through as chirps of Morse code.
>
> Then a signal cuts through.
>
> It's an SOS from a merchant ship. It gives her name, the *Ourang Medan*, and her position somewhere in the strait.
>
> An SOS sets off a chain of procedure. Every operator who hears it stops sending. They note the time. They write down every character exactly as it arrives, even the ones that make no sense. Because sometimes a single letter is the difference between finding a ship and losing it.
>
> And on this night, every single character mattered.

### Scene 2.2: The Message (09:31 – 11:09)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- A full-screen oscilloscope. Each Morse character becomes a pulse of red light that travels left to right and "prints" a letter into a typewriter line below.
- The message decodes in real time:
  `ALL OFFICERS INCLUDING CAPTAIN DEAD`
  `LYING IN CHARTROOM AND BRIDGE`
  `POSSIBLY WHOLE CREW DEAD`
- After each line, a short cutaway in the LEGEND palette: a quick vector flash of the room being described (the chartroom, the bridge), lit only by the flicker of the radio light.
- **Audio:** CW tones at a steady ~15 wpm, perfectly clean at first.

**Narration:**
> The receiving operators write it down, letter by letter.
>
> *All officers, including captain, dead. Lying in chartroom and bridge. Possibly whole crew dead.*
>
> Read that again, and think about who's sending it.
>
> This is the ship's radio operator. He's alone in a small cabin full of humming equipment. Somewhere above him, the captain and officers are dead. He doesn't know if anyone else is still alive. He doesn't know what killed them. And he's still at the key, sending a clean, professional message, because that's his job.
>
> Notice what he doesn't say. He doesn't say *fire*. He doesn't say *attack*, *pirates*, *collision* or *sinking*. A radio operator under attack would say so. A ship that was sinking would say so.
>
> Whatever is happening on the *Ourang Medan*, the operator either doesn't know what it is or can't put it into words. All he knows is that the men on the bridge are dead, and that it could be everyone.
>
> And look at the word *possibly*. *Possibly whole crew dead.* He hasn't checked. He hasn't gone down below. Maybe he can't. Maybe he's already too weak to stand.

- The waveform goes flat. A **[BEAT 0:12]** of silence holds on the flat line.

**Narration:**
> Then the rhythm changes.
>
> The receiving operators described a stretch of signal that no longer made sense. The spacing collapsed. Letters ran together. It was the sound of a hand losing control of the key.
>
> And then, clearly, two final words.
>
> *I die.*
>
> After that, the frequency went silent.
>
> Think about what that garbled stretch might mean. Remember the *fist*. An operator's rhythm doesn't collapse at random. It collapses when his hands stop obeying him: when he's shaking, when he can't see, when something is shutting his body down.
>
> If the story is true, those listening operators weren't just receiving a message. They were hearing a man die in real time, through the movement of his own hand.

## CHAPTER 3: THE APPROACH [14:11 – 19:40]

### Scene 3.1: Into the Dark (14:11 – 16:23)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- The bridge of the *Silver Star*, in LEGEND style. Silhouettes of the captain and officers against the windows, and a rain-streaked glass foreground layer.
- Over-the-shoulder shots of a man holding binoculars, then a cut to a binocular-shaped mask POV scanning empty sea.

- Lighting: red night-vision lamps on the bridge, the faint glow of the compass binnacle.
- **Audio:** the engine throb, the wind, a tight cello drone.

**Narration:**
> Think about what the crew of the *Silver Star* must have been imagining as they steamed through the night.
>
> Piracy was a real danger in these waters. So were wartime sea mines, which kept turning up in Asian shipping lanes for years after the war ended. A ship with a whole dead crew could mean an attack. It could mean disease. Plague ships weren't just an old sailors' tale.
>
> Whatever it was, it had killed the men on that ship quickly enough that a trained radio operator had time to send only a few lines before it killed him too.
>
> And now they were sailing straight toward it.
>
> Why go at all? Because the law of the sea, and centuries of seafaring tradition, say you do. A master who hears a distress call and is able to help is expected to go. Ignoring an SOS is one of the gravest failures a captain can commit.
>
> But there's a second, less noble reason to keep in mind. Under maritime salvage law, a crew that saves a ship in danger can claim a reward, a share of the value of the vessel and her cargo. And a ship whose entire crew is dead is, in effect, a derelict, about as far into "salvage" territory as a ship can get.
>
> So the men of the *Silver Star* weren't just rescuers. If there was nobody left to rescue, they were potentially looking at a prize. **[VERIFY: framing of salvage law; consult a maritime law source]**
>
> Hold on to that. It's going to explain a decision they make later.

### Scene 3.2: The Questions on the Bridge (16:23 – 17:33)
**Animation cue / visual description:**

- Quick insert "question cards" flip over the chart in white handwriting: *Disease? Gas? Attack? Cargo?*. Each card burns away at its edges as the next one appears.
- **Audio:** pencil scratch, a ticking chronometer, distant engine throb.

**Narration:**
> On the *Silver Star*'s bridge, the officers would have been trying to work out what they were walking into.
>
> If it was disease, sending men aboard could bring it back to their own ship. If it was gas, the boarding party could die the same way. If it was an attack, the attackers might still be nearby. If it was the cargo, whatever had killed the crew might still be in the hold.
>
> Every option was bad. And there was no way to know which one it was until someone stepped onto that deck.
>
> In the age before protective suits and gas detectors on merchant ships, the only test for whether a ship was safe to board was a human being. Somebody would have to go first.

**Narration:**
> They found her.
>

>
> And notice something. Her lifeboats are still in place.
>
> Whatever happened on that ship, nobody tried to abandon her. Whatever killed the crew, it didn't give them time to run.
>
> That one detail rules things out. A ship that's sinking gets abandoned. A ship on fire gets abandoned. A crew facing a disease spreading on board might try to reach land. Even men under attack try to run.
>
> These men didn't run. Either they didn't see it coming, or it was over before they could react.

## CHAPTER 4: THE BOARDING [19:40 – 27:47]

### Scene 4.1: The Deck (19:40 – 20:47)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- A full graphic-novel look: pure black, bone-white and blood-red accents only. Heavy ink lines.
- POV shot climbing the pilot ladder: hands grip rungs, rust flakes off. The camera crests the rail and the deck is revealed.
- A wide, slow pan across the deck. Fog curls around winches and hatch covers. Long shadows stretch from a low sun.
- Footsteps are visualized as subtle ripples of light across the steel plates.
- **Audio:** boots on steel ring hollow. No wind. No gulls. Unnatural quiet.

**Narration:**
> The first thing they noticed was the silence.
>
> A working ship is never quiet. Generators hum, pumps cycle, steel creaks and groans. Men shout. Even at anchor, a ship has a pulse.
>
> The *Ourang Medan* had none.
>
> No battle damage. No bullet holes. No smashed doors, no blood on the deck. Whatever had happened here, it hadn't been a fight.
>
> They called out. Nobody answered.
>
> They moved in pairs, as boarding parties do, because no one wanted to be alone on that ship. Every closed door was a question. Every shadow could be a man, alive or dead.
>
> They started toward the bridge, because that's where the answers on any ship are kept: the logbook, the charts, the captain.

### Scene 4.2: The Bridge (20:47 – 22:48)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- The boarding party approaches the bridge door. A tight close-up on a hand pushing it open. The door's hinges are a key sound moment.
- Inside, a flashlight beam sweeps the dim wheelhouse and the light catches **one figure at a time**:
  1. The captain, slumped near the wheel
  2. An officer on his back on the chartroom floor
  3. A hand frozen in mid-reach toward a chart table
- The bodies are stark white silhouettes against black. Faces are a few ink strokes only: wide eyes, open mouths. Never gore.
- Each reveal is a slow push-in, followed by a cut to the boarding party's faces with the flashlight bounce lighting their eyes.
- **[BEAT 0:10]**: hold on the wide shot, with no music.

**Narration:**
> They went to the bridge first.
>
> The captain was there. Dead. The officers lay in the wheelhouse and the chartroom, exactly where the radio message said they would be.
>
> And it was the *way* they lay that would haunt this story for decades.
>
> In the accounts that followed, the bodies were stiff, their faces turned upward, their mouths open and their eyes wide. Some had arms stretched out, as if reaching for something, or trying to push something away.
>
> The witnesses said they looked terrified.
>
> But there were no wounds. No blood. Not a mark on any of them.
>
> Now put yourself in the place of the men holding those flashlights. A ship's officer doesn't usually die on duty on his own bridge. And these men hadn't fallen over in their sleep, or been gathered and killed. They died where they stood, at their stations, doing their jobs, as if something had come for all of them at the same moment.
>
> And there's one question the story never really answers. What happened to the logbook? It should have been right there, on the bridge or in the chartroom: a written record of the ship's voyage, her ports and her cargo. In none of the famous versions does anyone pick it up. It never makes it off the ship.

### Scene 4.3: Below Decks (22:48 – 24:15)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- A tracking shot down a narrow corridor, with the flashlight beam as the only light source. Pipes run overhead, rendered as layered vectors in a slow parallax.
- Quick reveals in doorways: a crewman in a bunk, a man in a passageway, a figure in the engine-room shadows near the boilers.
- In the corridor, the beam finds a small shape on the floor: the **ship's dog**, its teeth bared. Hard rim light picks out the snarl. A snap-zoom with a red flash on the teeth.
- **Audio:** a single distant metallic groan from the hull. The boarding party's breathing gets louder.

**Narration:**
> Below decks, it was the same.
>
> Men in passageways. Men in their bunks. Men down in the engine spaces. All dead, and, according to the story, all with that same frozen look.
>
> Even the ship's dog was dead. They found it with its teeth bared, as if it had died snarling at something.
>
> Whatever it was, the animal had sensed it too.
>
> That detail, more than any other, has turned this story into horror. A dog doesn't believe in ghosts. A dog reacts to what it can sense. If it was snarling, something had set it off.
>
> Or, and here's the investigator's voice again, the bared teeth might be something else entirely. When the lips and facial muscles stiffen after death, an animal's teeth can be exposed. To frightened men in the dark, that would look like a snarl.

## CHAPTER 8: THE AUTOPSY THAT NEVER HAPPENED, SCIENTIFIC THEORIES [46:24 – 54:26]

### Scene 8.1: Theory One: The Mielke Cargo (46:24 – 48:06)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- A split-screen theory "dossier card" layout. Each card has a vector cutaway of the ship and a **FIT METER**, which shows how well the theory matches the *reported* details (not proof).
- Card 1: a cutaway of hold Nº 4. Seawater drips onto split drums labelled *KCN*. A pale vapour spreads through ventilation ducts toward the crew quarters, animated as particles.
- A second animation: a crate labelled *NG* (nitroglycerin) near flames, with a warning pulse.

**Narration:**
> Let's take the theories one at a time, as if we were the investigators who never got to examine this ship.
>
> First, Mielke's cargo. Potassium cyanide is a deadly industrial chemical. When it comes into contact with acids, it can release hydrogen cyanide gas, a fast-acting poison. If the containers broke open in the hold, maybe in rough seas, the gas could have spread through the ship's ventilation. Cyanide acts within minutes. A whole crew could have collapsed almost at once.
>
> And nitroglycerin is notoriously unstable. If a fire started on board, an explosion would be very likely.
>
> It fits the story almost perfectly. Maybe *too* perfectly, since this cargo first appears six years after the event, in a book that never explains where it came from.
>
> And there's a problem with the timing. If cyanide gas was spreading from the hold, the men nearest to it, the crew working below decks, should have died first. The officers on the bridge, high up and in fresh air, should have had the most time. But the message says the officers died first. That's not impossible if the gas was carried through the ventilation, but it's not what you'd expect.

### Scene 8.2: Theory Two: The Silent Killer in the Engine Room (48:06 – 50:03)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- Card 2: an engine-room cutaway. A cracked boiler flue. An invisible gas, shown as a faint heat-shimmer distortion, creeps through corridors and up stairways.
- A small medical inset: a red blood cell with oxygen molecules being pushed out by **CO** molecules.

**Narration:**
> Second, a far more ordinary killer: carbon monoxide.
>
> Old steamships burned coal or oil in their boilers. A cracked flue, a blocked vent or a smouldering fire below decks can fill a ship with carbon monoxide. It's colourless and has no smell. It binds to the blood far more strongly than oxygen does, and slowly starves the body. Victims get headaches, then confusion, then they collapse, often without realising what's happening.
>
> It would explain a crew dying with no wounds. And a fire that was already smouldering below decks could explain the blaze that came later.
>
> But it raises a question. If the gas crept up slowly, how did the radio operator know the officers were dead, and still have the clarity to send that message? Maybe the radio room was the last place the gas reached. Maybe that's why he was the last to die.
>
> And notice how well carbon monoxide fits the garbled signal. Confusion and loss of coordination are classic signs of carbon monoxide poisoning. An operator whose fist falls apart before he sends his last two words is exactly what you'd expect.
>
> There's one more detail in its favour. Carbon monoxide poisoning is known for turning the skin of its victims an unusual pinkish-red colour. No one in the story mentions that. But then again, men looking at bodies by flashlight in the dark might never have noticed.

### Scene 8.3: The Faces (50:03 – 51:26)

> On the bridge, the officers feel it too. Confused, they don't realise what's happening until they collapse.
>
> In the radio room, the operator realises something is very wrong. He goes looking, finds the officers dead, and gets back to his set. He starts sending. And as the gas keeps building, his fist starts falling apart, until he manages two final words.
>
> Hours later, the rescue party boards. The bodies are stiff with rigor mortis. The faces are frozen. The fire in the hold is still smouldering, and then, with the hatch disturbed or air finally getting in, it flares. Whatever is in the cargo goes up.
>
> Is that what happened? We can't know. But notice: this version needs no nerve agents, no ghosts and no aliens. Just a fire, a gas and a crew who didn't realise they were dying.
>
> And yet, even this explanation rests on the parts of the story we can't prove.

### Scene 9.2: Two Possible Ships (55:13 – 57:10)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- The screen splits vertically. On the left, the *Ourang Medan* in amber: a real, rusting, unregistered smuggler's freighter running dark through the blockade. On the right, the same ship in red, made of floating newspaper text that reassembles itself into a hull.
- The two images slowly drift toward each other and merge in the middle into a single cold-blue ghost ship.
- **Audio:** the haunting piano motif begins, under ocean waves.

**Narration:**
> In the end, there are two possible ships.
>
> The first is a real ship. A rusting freighter that was never supposed to appear on paper, running dark through the blockades of a region at war, carrying a cargo nobody would ever admit to. Something on board killed her crew, whether gas, fire or poison, and the people who knew what she was carrying made sure the world forgot she existed.
>
> The second is a ship made entirely of words. A rumour from the chaos of the post-war years that passed from newspaper to bulletin to book, picking up a detail at every step, until it became one of the most famous mysteries of the sea.
>
> And here's the thing. We can't prove which one she is.
>
> Not because the evidence was destroyed in an explosion. But because, if she was real, it was never supposed to exist in the first place.
>
> And maybe that's the most unsettling part. Either a crew of men died terribly and the world simply let them disappear, with no names, no graves and no one asking where they went. Or we built a legend so convincing that, seventy years later, millions of people still mourn a ship that was never there.
>
> Both answers say something about us. About how easily real people can vanish from history. And about how easily invented ones can take their place.

### Scene 9.3: What Would Settle It (57:10 – 58:41)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- A sonar-sweep animation over the seabed of the strait: a green radial scan revealing scattered wreck silhouettes, dozens of them, each with a faint label (*Unidentified*, *WWII*, *Unknown*).
- Then an amber archive drawer slides open, revealing stacks of uncatalogued paper files and reels of microfilm. A cursor blinks in a search bar: *"Ourang Medan"*.

**Narration:**
> So what would it take to solve this?
>
> Two things.
>
> The first is in the water. The Strait of Malacca is full of wrecks: merchant ships, warships, and vessels sunk in the Second World War, many of them never identified. If the *Ourang Medan* exploded and sank, she's down there somewhere. A wreck with the right size, damage and location, and some identifying mark on her hull or bell, would turn this legend into history overnight.
>
> The second is in the archives. Shipping records, colonial radio logs, naval patrol reports and port files from the late 1940s. Many of these exist in national archives in the Netherlands, Britain, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore and the United States. Plenty of them have never been fully digitised or searched with this story in mind.
>
> Somewhere in one of those archives, there may be a single log entry, a single line written by a radio operator on duty one night, that tells us whether a message was ever received at all.
>
> Until someone finds it, the case stays open.

### Scene 9.4: The Last Transmission (58:41 – 59:22)
**Animation cue / visual description:**
- The camera rises from the strait, pulling back through the fog layers and clouds into night sky, until the Earth is a small blue curve. The motion is slow and continuous, with no cuts.
- In the last few seconds, a single faint red pulse blinks on the dark water far below: dot, dot, dot.
- Cut to black.
- **Audio:** the piano resolves, the waves fade, then **3 seconds of total silence**. Then one final, quiet Morse pulse.

**Narration:**
> The *Ourang Medan* remains one of history's great maritime ghosts. Perhaps she lies on the floor of the Strait of Malacca, rusting quietly in the dark. Perhaps she only ever sailed on paper.
>
> But either way, those two words are still out there, crossing the decades like a signal that never fades.
>
> *I die.*
>
> Until next time, stay curious.

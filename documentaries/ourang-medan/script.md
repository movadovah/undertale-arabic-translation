# THE GHOST SHIP OF THE STRAITS: The True Mystery of the SS Ourang Medan

**Format:** Full-length documentary video essay (target runtime: ~60 minutes)
**Style guide:** Fast-paced and cinematic. Dark vector art, high-contrast lighting, rapid motion graphics, dynamic typography, atmospheric sound design.
**Narration pace:** about 140 words per minute, with music and visual-only beats in between (marked **[BEAT]**, with durations).

---

## 0. Production notes: read before recording

### 0.1 How the story is told
The original outline told the legend as plain fact. Every detail the audience remembers (the Morse message, the dog, the cold, the explosion) comes from **second- and third-hand retellings**. None of it comes from an official record. A documentary that calls itself *"The True Mystery"* gets its credibility, and its strongest twist, from showing that gap.

So the script works on **two tracks**:
- **THE LEGEND**: told straight, at full cinematic intensity (Acts I–III).
- **THE RECORD**: what the paper trail can and cannot support (Acts IV–V).

Visual rule: legend scenes use the **blood-red** accent. Scenes about the record use **archival amber**. The audience should always be able to tell which track they're on.

### 0.2 Corrections to the original outline

| Original draft | Issue | Fix in this script |
|---|---|---|
| Distress call as a *"distorted human voice"*, *"breathless... gurgling"* | Every version says the message came by **Morse code (radio telegraphy)**, not voice. Gurgling can't be heard in Morse. | The SOS is dots and dashes. The "voice" becomes a recreation of the operator's keying going erratic. |
| *City of Baltimore* as a receiving ship | It doesn't appear in the core versions of the story. | Cut. Receivers are Dutch and British listening posts plus passing ships, with the *Silver Star* as the boarding ship. |
| *"February 1948"* as a fixed date | Versions disagree: June 1947, late 1947, February 1948. | The date is presented as disputed. That uncertainty becomes part of the story. |
| *"Temperature plummeted below freezing"* | This shows up only in later embellished retellings, not in the earliest accounts. | Kept as legend, flagged on screen as a later addition. |
| *"Vaporizing itself"* | The accounts say the ship burned, exploded and **sank**. | "Exploded and sank." |
| Act IV narration describes the bodies | That belongs to the boarding scene (Act III). Act IV is the investigation. | The bodies description moved to Act III. Act IV rewritten as the investigation. |
| Each act had ~100 words of narration | 60 minutes needs roughly 7,000–8,500 words. | Narration written for every scene (~2,500 words so far). See Appendix B for the remaining gap. |
| *"Sarin"* stated as a theory | No source links sarin specifically. "Nerve agents" is the speculative claim. | Framed as "a nerve or chemical agent", clearly marked as speculation. |
| Fake *CIA/MI6* stamps | These suggest real documents exist. They don't. | Stamps used only as stylized art, with an on-screen disclaimer the first time they appear. |

### 0.3 Fact-check flags
Lines marked **[VERIFY]** depend on secondary research. Confirm them against primary scans before final lock: the newspaper archives Delpher (KB, Netherlands) and Trove, and the US Coast Guard *Proceedings of the Merchant Marine Council*, May 1952.

## [00:00 – 05:00] ACT I: THE HOOK

### Scene 1.1: The Silence of the Straits (00:00 – 02:30)
* **Visual:** Dark, moody 2.5D vector art of the Strait of Malacca at night. Deep navy, pitch-black water, rolling fog in parallax layers. A slow, creeping drone push over the waves. Lightning flashes show a single merchant ship drifting with no running lights.
* **Typography:** Glitch-in title card: *"The Strait of Malacca. 1947? 1948?"* The question marks flicker in and out. This sets up the date dispute.
* **Audio:** A low-frequency hum builds into a metallic screech, then cuts to silence.
* **[BEAT 0:10]**: just water and wind.
* **Narration:**
  > Imagine floating in the middle of a vast, indifferent ocean. The night is pitch black. The air is thick and wet and hot, the kind of heat that never really leaves the equator, not even at midnight.
  >
  > And then the radio comes alive.
  >
  > Not with a voice. With a rhythm. Short pulses and long ones, dots and dashes, tapped out by a human hand somewhere out there in the dark. You've heard a thousand of these messages: weather reports, position checks, routine traffic between merchant ships crossing one of the busiest waterways on Earth.
  >
  > This one is different. This one is a message of pure terror.
  >
  > It will be retold for more than seventy years, in newspapers, paperbacks, late-night radio shows, and an endless scroll of internet videos. It will be called the most disturbing maritime mystery ever recorded.
  >
  > And it comes from a ship that, as far as anyone has been able to prove, never existed.

### Scene 1.2: The Cold Open Montage (02:30 – 04:00)
* **Visual:** Rapid-fire montage, one image per beat, each cut on a percussion hit: a Morse key → a dead man's hand on a chart table → a dog's bared teeth → fire curling out of a cargo hatch → a ship's silhouette sliding under the surface.
* **Typography:** Each image gets a one-word label slammed in: *SOS. DEAD. FROZEN. FIRE. GONE.*
* **Narration:**
  > A distress call that ends mid-sentence. A boarding party that finds every crew member dead, with no visible wounds. A ship's dog frozen in a snarl. A fire in the cargo hold. An explosion. And then nothing: no wreck, no bodies, no inquiry, no owner, no paperwork.
  >
  > The name of the ship was the *Ourang Medan*.

### Scene 1.3: The Promise (04:00 – 05:00)
* **Visual:** The title card assembles from shards of a radio waveform: **THE GHOST SHIP OF THE STRAITS.** Below it, two labels split apart, one red and one amber: **THE LEGEND / THE RECORD.**
* **Narration:**
  > Tonight we're going to tell this story twice. First the way it has always been told, every chilling detail. Then we're going to follow the paper trail back as far as it goes, and find out where it really leads.
  >
  > Because the real mystery of the *Ourang Medan* isn't only what killed its crew. It's how a ship that may never have sailed became one of the most famous wrecks in the world.

## [05:00 – 18:00] ACT II: THE INTERCEPT

### Scene 2.1: The Crossroads of the World (05:00 – 08:30)
* **Visual:** An animated map. Pull back from the strait to show Sumatra on one side and the Malay Peninsula on the other, with Singapore at the southern tip. Shipping lanes pulse like arteries. Overlay: 1940s port names (Batavia, Medan/Belawan, Penang, Singapore).
* **Typography:** Stat cards: *~900 km long. At its narrowest, under 3 km wide (Phillips Channel, Singapore Strait).* **[VERIFY]**
* **Audio:** Ships' horns, a busy harbour, all heavily reverbed.
* **Narration:**
  > To understand why this story landed so hard, you have to understand where it's set.
  >
  > The Strait of Malacca is a long, narrow channel between the Indonesian island of Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula. It has been one of the world's great trade routes for well over a thousand years. Spices, silk, tin, rubber, oil: almost everything moving between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea has passed through this funnel of water.
  >
  > And in the years right after the Second World War, it was a dangerous, chaotic place.
  >
  > The Japanese occupation had just ended. The Dutch were trying to take back their colony in the East Indies, and Indonesian nationalists had declared independence and were fighting them for it. British forces were still in Malaya. Navies blockaded. Smugglers ran guns, opium, rubber and fuel past the patrols. Wartime stockpiles of weapons and chemicals were scattered across the region, and nobody had a complete list of them.
  >
  > It was a place where ships could vanish. Where cargo didn't always match the manifest. Where a ship with no traceable owner wouldn't have seemed impossible.
  >
  > Remember that. It matters later.

### Scene 2.2: Decoding the Static (08:30 – 12:30)
* **Visual:** A retro-futuristic overlay of a 1940s radio room, mixed with sleek modern data graphics. Flickering green CRT glow. A Morse key in close-up. Each dot and dash becomes a glowing pulse on an oscilloscope, which then decodes letter by letter into red type.
* **Decoded text on screen (verbatim from the commonly quoted version):**
  `ALL OFFICERS INCLUDING CAPTAIN DEAD LYING IN CHARTROOM AND BRIDGE. POSSIBLY WHOLE CREW DEAD.`
  → A burst of garbled, unreadable Morse, shown as broken glyphs →
  `I DIE.`
* **Audio:** Real-sounding CW (Morse) tones at about 15 wpm. They get faster and sloppier, the spacing collapses, and then they stop. A long hiss of empty carrier, then silence. **No voice.**
* **[BEAT 0:15]**: the final `I DIE.` holds on screen in silence.
* **Narration:**
  > As the legend tells it, the first signals were picked up by listening posts on shore, Dutch and British stations along the strait, and by merchant ships in the area.
  >
  > The message was an SOS. It identified the sender as the *Ourang Medan*, a Dutch freighter. The operator gave the ship's position.
  >
  > Then came the words that have echoed for decades: *"All officers including captain dead, lying in chartroom and bridge. Possibly whole crew dead."*
  >
  > Picture what that means for the person sending it. He's alone in the radio room. Somewhere above him, the men who run the ship are dead. And he's still keying, still doing his job, still trying to get word out.
  >
  > Then the rhythm breaks.
  >
  > The receiving operators described a stretch of garbled signal, dots and dashes that no longer spelled anything. A hand losing control of the key.
  >
  > And then two final words came through clearly.
  >
  > *"I die."*
  >
  > After that, the frequency went silent.

### Scene 2.3: The Race to the Coordinates (12:30 – 15:30)
* **Visual:** A tactical map in amber. Triangulation lines from the shore stations meet at a pulsing point in the strait. A single ship icon, labelled ***SILVER STAR***, turns toward it. A clock counter ticks in the corner.
* **Typography:** *"Triangulated position"*, then a small amber footnote: *Coordinates vary between versions.*
* **Narration:**
  > The operators did what radio operators do: they triangulated the signal, roughly fixing the sender's position from where the different stations heard it.
  >
  > The nearest ship, according to the story, was an American merchant vessel called the *Silver Star*. She changed course and headed for the coordinates at full speed.
  >
  > On board, the crew would have had hours to imagine what they were sailing into. Pirates were a real threat in these waters. So were mines left from the war. A ship whose officers were all dead and whose radio operator was dying sounded like a crime scene, or a plague ship, or something worse.
  >
  > When the *Silver Star* reached the position, the lookouts found her: a merchant ship lying still in the water, apparently undamaged, with no one on deck. No one answered their signals.

### Scene 2.4: The Problem Nobody Mentions Yet (15:30 – 18:00)
* **Visual:** A freeze-frame. Everything drains to greyscale except a single amber note that slides in from the edge, like a researcher's sticky note: *"Which listening posts? Which operators? Where are the logs?"*
* **Audio:** A tick of a clock, then the music resumes.
* **Narration:**
  > Before we go aboard, a quick pause, because this is where a careful listener should feel the first itch.
  >
  > In most retellings, the listening posts have no names. The operators have no names. No one can produce a log entry with a timestamp. The *Silver Star*'s own crew, who supposedly saw everything that follows, never give a single first-hand interview in any of the well-known versions.
  >
  > Hold that thought. We'll come back to it.
  >
  > For now, let's go aboard.

## [18:00 – 35:00] ACT III: THE BOARDING PARTY

### Scene 3.1: Stepping Aboard the Floating Tomb (18:00 – 22:30)
* **Visual:** A cinematic graphic-novel look: stark black and white with a single blood-red accent. A dynamic pan follows the boarding party as they row across and climb a rusted pilot ladder. Fog rolls over the deck. Boots hit steel plating in tight close-ups.
* **Lighting:** Harsh flashlight beams cut through the dark.
* **Audio:** Heavy breathing, oars, then echoing footsteps on steel. A low synth drone tightens under it.
* **Narration:**
  > A small boarding party lowered a boat and rowed across. They climbed up the side and stepped onto the deck of the *Ourang Medan*.
  >
  > They didn't find a battle. No bullet holes, no smashed doors, no blood. They didn't find signs of a mutiny. The ship looked intact.
  >
  > What they found, the story says, was a tomb.

### Scene 3.2: The Bridge (22:30 – 26:30)
* **Visual:** The bridge doors open. A slow reveal, with flashlight beams landing on one figure at a time: a man slumped against the wheel, an officer on the floor of the chartroom, a hand still reaching for something. Faces are stylized and never gory: wide eyes and open mouths drawn in stark silhouette.
* **Typography:** None. Let the image carry it.
* **[BEAT 0:10]**: silence as the beam moves.
* **Narration:**
  > The captain was found on the bridge. The officers were in the chartroom and the wheelhouse, exactly where the radio message said they would be.
  >
  > Every one of them was dead.
  >
  > And it wasn't just that they were dead. It was *how* they looked. In the accounts that would be repeated for decades, their bodies were rigid, their faces turned up toward the sun, their mouths open, their eyes wide. Some had arms stretched out as if reaching for something, or pushing something away. The rescuers described them as looking terrified.
  >
  > There were no wounds. No blood. No sign that anyone had fought anyone else.

### Scene 3.3: The Dog and the Radio Room (26:30 – 30:00)
* **Visual:** Below decks. A narrow corridor, with a flashlight showing a small shape on the floor: the ship's dog, teeth bared, frozen mid-snarl. Cut to the radio room. The operator is slumped at his set, his hand still resting on the Morse key. A red light glows on the transmitter.
* **Audio:** A single, distant metallic creak of the hull. The faint ghost of the Morse rhythm from Act II, played backwards.
* **Narration:**
  > Below decks, the scene repeated itself. Crew members in passageways, in the engine spaces, in their quarters. All dead. All, supposedly, with that same frozen expression.
  >
  > Even the ship's dog was dead, found with its teeth bared, frozen in a snarl.
  >
  > And then the boarding party reached the radio room.
  >
  > The operator was there, slumped at his set. In the most famous version, his hand was still on the telegraph key. The man who had sent *"I die"* had done exactly that, at his post.

### Scene 3.4: The Cold (30:00 – 32:00)
* **Visual:** Frost creeps across the frame from the edges. Then the image glitches, and an amber annotation stamps over it: **LATER ADDITION.**
* **Narration:**
  > Some retellings add one more detail, maybe the most unsettling of all. They say that even in the blistering tropical heat of the strait, the men felt a deep, unnatural chill on board. Some versions claim it was freezing cold.
  >
  > You'll find that detail in countless videos and articles. But you won't find it in the earliest accounts. It's a good example of something that happens to every great story: it picks up details as it travels. Keep an eye out for that, because it happens again and again with this ship.

### Scene 3.5: Fire in Number Four (32:00 – 35:00)
* **Visual:** The boarding party back on deck. A thin plume of smoke rises from a cargo hatch, then thick black smoke. Hard cuts: men running, ropes being cut, oars pulling. A wide shot: the *Silver Star* backing away. The *Ourang Medan* erupts in a fireball, lists, and slides under, leaving a burning slick and silence.
* **Audio:** Crackling fire builds into a massive low-end explosion, then a high-pitched ringing tone that fades to just water.
* **[BEAT 0:20]**: the burning slick on dark water, no narration.
* **Narration:**
  > The *Silver Star*'s crew decided to tow the ship to port. Whatever had happened here, investigators would need to see it.
  >
  > They began rigging a towline. And that's when someone noticed the smoke.
  >
  > It was coming from below decks, from the cargo hold. Most versions say it was the Number Four hold. The fire spread fast. The boarding party scrambled back to their boat and cut the tow just in time.
  >
  > Moments later, the *Ourang Medan* exploded.
  >
  > The blast was violent enough that the ship went down quickly. She sank in the strait, taking every body, every log book and every scrap of cargo with her.
  >
  > Whatever killed her crew was now at the bottom of the sea.
  >
  > That's the legend. That's the story as it has been told a thousand times.
  >
  > Now let's try to prove it.

## [35:00 – 48:00] ACT IV: THE INVESTIGATION

### Scene 4.1: The Ship That Isn't There (35:00 – 38:30)
* **Visual:** An investigative corkboard look in archival amber. A dark room, a desk lamp, sharp shadows. A thick ledger titled **LLOYD'S REGISTER** opens. A magnifying glass sweeps down columns of ship names, and the "O" section shows no *Ourang Medan*. A rubber stamp hits: **NO RECORD.**
* **On-screen disclaimer (first use of stamps):** *Stamps and redactions in this film are artistic recreations, not real documents.*
* **Audio:** A ticking clock that slowly speeds up. Paper shuffling.
* **Narration:**
  > If a ship sinks with all hands, it leaves a trail. There's a registry entry showing who owned her and where she was built. There are insurance claims. There are crew contracts and grieving families. There's an official inquiry, because governments and insurers want to know why a ship was lost.
  >
  > For the *Ourang Medan*, researchers have searched for that trail for decades.
  >
  > The ship doesn't appear in Lloyd's Register, the standard reference listing merchant ships of the time. **[VERIFY]** No Dutch registry entry has been found. No owner has been identified. No insurance claim. No crew list. No inquiry.
  >
  > Even the name is odd. *Orang Medan* in Malay means "man from Medan" or "person from Medan," Medan being the big city of North Sumatra. That's a believable name for a ship working those waters. It's also exactly the kind of name a writer might invent to make a story sound local.

### Scene 4.2: Following the Paper Trail (38:30 – 43:00)
* **Visual:** Newspaper clippings fly onto the board, each tagged with a year, from earliest to latest. Red string connects them in a chain. Each new clipping adds a detail that appears as a small icon, like the dog, the cold, or the cargo, so you can see the story growing.
* **Typography:** A running "Detail Counter" in the corner ticks up with each clipping.
* **Narration:**
  > So if there's no official trail, where does the story come from?
  >
  > Researchers, including the Dutch writer Theo Paijmans, have traced it through old newspapers. **[VERIFY]** The earliest known printed versions appeared in Dutch-language newspapers in the Indies and the Netherlands around 1948. They describe the distress call and the dead crew, but in less detail than the later versions.
  >
  > The version most English-language retellings come from appeared in May 1952, in the *Proceedings of the Merchant Marine Council*, a publication of the United States Coast Guard. **[VERIFY]** Because it carried the Coast Guard's name, it gave the story an official feel. But it wasn't an investigation report. It was a retelling.
  >
  > Then, in 1954, a German author named Otto Mielke published a book about mysteries of the sea. **[VERIFY]** His version adds the most specific details yet: a Chinese port of departure, and a dangerous illegal cargo of potassium cyanide and nitroglycerin. Those details would become the backbone of almost every theory that followed. But it's not clear where Mielke got them.
  >
  > And that's the pattern. With each retelling, the story gets more vivid: the dog, the faces, the frozen hand on the key, the cold. The details pile up. The sources don't.

### Scene 4.3: The Missing Witnesses (43:00 – 45:30)
* **Visual:** Silhouette cut-outs of "witnesses" (a radio operator, a *Silver Star* crewman, a port officer) placed on the board. One by one, each has a red question mark stamped over it.
* **Narration:**
  > Think about everyone who should have been able to confirm this.
  >
  > The radio operators at the listening posts: no named operator has come forward in any of the core accounts. The crew of the *Silver Star*: no confirmed first-hand interview has surfaced. **[VERIFY]** The port authorities who'd have had to deal with a ship disappearing in their waters: nothing.
  >
  > An event this dramatic, witnessed by dozens of sailors, would normally leave a mark: a newspaper photo, a memoir, a family story, a dockside rumour that someone wrote down.
  >
  > Instead, we have a story that seems to have arrived fully formed, with no one standing behind it.

### Scene 4.4: Why Not Just Call It a Hoax? (45:30 – 48:00)
* **Visual:** The corkboard pulls back, showing how tangled the strings are. Then the map from Act II returns, with the smuggling routes, blockade lines and wartime stockpiles all glowing.
* **Narration:**
  > So is that the answer? Was it all made up?
  >
  > Maybe. But it's not quite that simple, and that's what keeps the *Ourang Medan* alive.
  >
  > Remember where we started: a strait full of smugglers, blockade runners and ships sailing under false names, in the middle of a war of independence, with leftover weapons and chemicals all over the region.
  >
  > In that world, a ship with no registry entry doesn't automatically mean a ship that never existed. It could mean a ship that was never supposed to be on the books. A ship whose owners would have had every reason to make sure no one asked about her.
  >
  > Which brings us to the real question. If something like this did happen, what could actually have killed an entire crew in minutes, without a mark on their bodies?

## [48:00 – 60:00] ACT V: WHAT REALLY HAPPENED?

### Scene 5.1: The Theories (48:00 – 55:00)
* **Visual:** A split-screen data graphic. Each theory gets its own panel with a vector diagram (a molecule, a cutaway of the engine room, a cargo hold). Each panel has a "plausibility meter" showing **how well it fits the reported details**. This is not a claim about what happened.
* **Audio:** A pulse beat under the theory cards. Each card arrives on a hit.

#### Theory 1: Toxic cargo (potassium cyanide and nitroglycerin)
* **Visual:** A cutaway of the hold. Water drips onto damaged drums, and a pale gas spreads through the ventilation.
* **Narration:**
  > The most popular theory comes straight from Mielke's version: the ship was smuggling a cargo of potassium cyanide and nitroglycerin.
  >
  > Potassium cyanide can release hydrogen cyanide gas when it reacts with moisture and acids. If containers broke open, maybe in a storm or with seawater getting into the hold, toxic gas could have spread through the ship's spaces. Cyanide works fast, and it would explain a crew dying close together.
  >
  > Nitroglycerin is notoriously unstable. A fire on board would make an explosion very likely, which would explain the ending.
  >
  > It's a neat theory. Maybe too neat. The cargo comes from a source that appeared six years after the event, with no clear origin of its own.

#### Theory 2: Carbon monoxide
* **Visual:** An engine-room cutaway. A cracked boiler flue, with a clear, invisible gas shown as a faint shimmer filling the corridors.
* **Narration:**
  > A less dramatic idea: carbon monoxide. A faulty boiler or a fire smouldering below decks could fill the ship with a colourless, odourless gas. People poisoned by carbon monoxide often feel headaches and confusion before they collapse, and they may not realise what's happening.
  >
  > It would explain a crew dying without wounds. It's harder to square with a radio operator who knew everyone was dead and had time to send a message about it, but a slower exposure in the radio room might allow for that.

#### Theory 3: A secret chemical weapons shipment
* **Visual:** A grey molecular structure labelled only *"nerve/chemical agent"*. Stylized barrels marked with stencilled skulls. Amber footnote: *Speculative. No document links the Ourang Medan to chemical weapons.*
* **Narration:**
  > The darkest theory says the ship was secretly carrying chemical weapons: leftover wartime stock being moved, sold or dumped. This isn't fantasy in general. After the war, large amounts of chemical weapons really were disposed of at sea, and stockpiles existed across Asia. **[VERIFY]**
  >
  > A leak of a nerve or chemical agent could kill a crew quickly. It would also explain why no one wanted to own the ship afterwards, and why there'd be no paper trail.
  >
  > But there's no document linking the *Ourang Medan* to any weapons programme. This theory fits the silence perfectly. The problem is that it fits the silence because it has nothing else to stand on.

#### Theory 4: The supernatural and the unknown
* **Visual:** The panels darken. Fog spreads across the split screen. Rough sketches of the theories people have raised over the years: ghosts, a "cursed ship", even UFOs.
* **Narration:**
  > Then there are the stranger explanations: ghosts, curses, even aliens. They're popular because the story's terrified faces seem to cry out for something more than chemistry.
  >
  > But it's worth remembering that the faces are the least reliable part of the story. They come from retellings, not from an autopsy. Sudden death can also leave bodies rigid and faces fixed in an expression. The terror may be something we added to them.

### Scene 5.2: The Real Answer (55:00 – 58:00)
* **Visual:** All the theory panels slide away. The red legend track and the amber record track from the title card come back and slowly merge into a single cold blue.
* **Narration:**
  > So what really happened?
  >
  > The honest answer is that we don't know, and we may never know. But we can say something more useful than that.
  >
  > If the *Ourang Medan* was real, she was most likely a ship that wasn't supposed to exist on paper. Maybe a smuggler, maybe something worse, lost in the chaos of a region at war, killed by a cargo or a malfunction that left her crew no time to escape.
  >
  > If she wasn't real, then she's something just as interesting: a story that began somewhere in the newspapers of 1948, passed from writer to writer, and gained a little more detail at every step, until it became one of the most famous mysteries of the sea.
  >
  > Either way, the paper trail tells us what kind of story this is. It's a story about how fear spreads. About how a few lines of Morse code, real or imagined, can echo for more than seventy years.

### Scene 5.3: Outro (58:00 – 60:00)
* **Visual:** The camera pulls back from the strait, past the clouds and up into the night sky, until the Earth is a small blue light in the dark. A melodic piano motif plays over the ocean sound.
* **[BEAT 0:15]**: piano and waves only.
* **Narration:**
  > The *Ourang Medan* remains one of history's great maritime ghosts. Maybe she lies on the floor of the strait. Maybe she only ever sailed on paper.
  >
  > And somewhere in the dark currents of the Strait of Malacca, if the story is true, a radio key is still waiting for the next message.
  >
  > Until next time, stay curious.
* **Audio:** The piano fades, then the waves fade. Total silence for three seconds. Cut to black.

**Honest gap:** the narration as written is about **18 minutes** of speech. With the marked visual beats and normal documentary breathing room, that's roughly a **25-minute cut**. A true 60-minute runtime needs about 3x the narration. Where to expand, once the Appendix A sources are verified:
- **Act III** has the biggest gap. Add the *Silver Star*'s approach in real time, the boarding party's decisions and the tow attempt, and keep the legend detail-for-detail with the earliest text you can verify.
- **Scene 2.1:** the post-war situation in the region and the Indonesian independence war (1945–49).
- **Scene 4.2:** read the actual newspaper and *Proceedings* texts on screen, side by side, to show the story growing.
- **Scene 5.1:** an expert interview on cyanide and carbon monoxide poisoning and on post-mortem rigidity (cadaveric spasm).

Alternatively, re-target the film at **~25–30 minutes**, which suits the current material and the fast-paced style.
